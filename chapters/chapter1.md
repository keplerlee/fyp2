# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background

Insomnia affects both sleep and daytime functioning, making the delivery of accessible, evidence-based support an important concern. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) addresses behavioral patterns and beliefs that maintain sleep difficulties. Its components include sleep restriction, stimulus control, sleep hygiene education, and cognitive restructuring. A review of psychological and behavioral treatments supports the effectiveness of these approaches (Morin et al., 2006). A dismantling trial also demonstrates the value of combining behavioral components rather than assuming that a single component provides an equivalent intervention (Epstein et al., 2012).

CBT-I requires more than presenting general information about sleep. Behavioral activities depend on recorded sleep patterns, decisions about time in bed, and progress across repeated sessions. Cognitive work addresses beliefs such as rigid expectations about sleep duration, catastrophic predictions about daytime performance, and perceived helplessness. A conversational system intended to support these activities must therefore maintain an orderly workflow while responding appropriately to concerns that arise during an interaction.

Internet-delivered CBT-I (iCBT-I) offers a way to deliver structured intervention content beyond face-to-face appointments. Automated online programs have been evaluated in randomized trials, including the web application studied by Espie et al. (2012). Ritterband et al. (2017) report outcomes from a web-based intervention with a one-year follow-up. These studies establish a basis for investigating digital delivery, although their findings do not demonstrate the effectiveness of every subsequent digital system or conversational architecture.

Structured applications can support diaries and established intervention activities. For example, CBT-I Coach has been investigated as an adjunct for patients receiving CBT-I, with attention to feasibility and acceptability (Koffel et al., 2018). Such tools illustrate the usefulness of explicitly represented tasks. However, a conversational interface introduces a further requirement: the system must interpret varied user messages while preserving the data and rules needed for the current task. Merely offering a free-text input does not resolve that requirement.

Large language models (LLMs) provide a means of generating context-sensitive responses and interpreting natural language. Their potential role in insomnia support has motivated work on dialogue systems and adaptation strategies, including the comparative evaluation by Bao et al. (2025). Nevertheless, fluent responses alone cannot establish that a workflow follows its intended protocol. When generation also controls stage progression, numerical calculations, and the selection of intervention content, errors in any of these responsibilities may be concealed within an apparently coherent reply.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) connects response generation to information retrieved from an external collection (Lewis et al., 2020). In this project, retrieval is intended to provide a traceable source of behavioral guidance and cognitive restructuring material. Retrieval does not itself define when a stage is complete, whether diary inputs are valid, or how a paused task should resume. These responsibilities require an explicit control mechanism alongside the generation and retrieval components.

The FYP1 proposal therefore motivates a hybrid RAG-FSM architecture. A hierarchical finite state machine (FSM) manages behavioral progression, a deterministic calculator handles sleep-related arithmetic, and two separate knowledge bases support behavioral and cognitive content. A cognitive interrupt mechanism allows the system to address a relevant dysfunctional sleep belief and then return to the interrupted task. The present study develops and evaluates this architecture as a software prototype, focusing on observable system behavior and preliminary interface usability.

## 1.2 Problem Statement and Justification

The central problem is how to combine flexible natural-language interaction with dependable execution of a structured iCBT-I workflow. The behavioral and cognitive parts of the proposed system have different control requirements. Behavioral tasks require defined prerequisites, persistent diary data, and guarded transitions. Cognitive restructuring requires context-sensitive dialogue that may be needed before the current behavioral task is complete. Treating both activities as an unrestricted sequence of generated responses makes their interaction difficult to inspect and test.

The first architectural concern is workflow drift. A user may provide incomplete diary information, ask an unrelated question, or request guidance associated with a later stage. The system needs to distinguish a valid transition from a conversational detour. Progress should be determined by the defined rules and available data, rather than by the model's tendency to continue a plausible conversation. This motivates an FSM layer in which states, transition conditions, and retained variables are explicitly represented.

The second concern is numerical consistency. Sleep diary interpretation and sleep-window calculations involve quantities such as total sleep time, time in bed, and sleep efficiency. A generated explanation must not become the authoritative source of these calculations. The prototype therefore requires a deterministic calculation component with validated inputs and independently specified expected outputs. Agreement with that component is a software correctness criterion; it does not establish that a calculated sleep window is appropriate for an individual patient.

The third concern is the separation of behavioral and cognitive knowledge. Behavioral retrieval should supply material relevant to the permitted workflow activity, while cognitive retrieval should supply material relevant to an identified belief. If the two retrieval routes are mixed without clear boundaries, responses may include content inappropriate to the active task. Separate knowledge bases and explicit routing provide a testable design for controlling which material each component may use. The effectiveness of this separation still needs to be evaluated through retrieval-isolation tests.

The fourth concern is interruption and recovery. A statement expressing a dysfunctional sleep belief may justify temporarily suspending a behavioral task, whereas ordinary tiredness, grief, or a factual question may not justify cognitive restructuring. Both missed triggers and unnecessary triggers can disrupt the interaction. After an interrupt, the system must recover the appropriate behavioral state and its associated data. A response that simply announces a return to the task is insufficient if the underlying state or pending information has been lost.

Together, these concerns motivate an architectural investigation of explicit state control, separate retrieval routes, deterministic calculations, and checkpoint-based recovery. The study tests whether this combination improves the specified behaviors relative to a linear LLM-RAG baseline using the same model and knowledge resources. Its research gap is framed as the need to evaluate this particular integration for the selected iCBT-I tasks. It does not assume that a hybrid architecture guarantees clinical safety, eliminates hallucinations, or constitutes the first possible implementation of every individual component.

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives

The aim of this project is to design, develop, and evaluate an iCBT-I dialogue-system prototype that integrates RAG with hierarchical FSM orchestration to support consistent behavioral workflow execution and context-sensitive cognitive restructuring. The emphasis is on the reliability of the software architecture under specified test conditions.

Objective 1 is to develop an integrated prototype comprising a LangGraph-based FSM, separate behavioral and cognitive retrieval pipelines, persistent dialogue state, and a conversational interface. Completion will be assessed through end-to-end scenarios demonstrating that the components exchange the required data and that each retrieval route uses its designated knowledge base.

Objective 2 is to implement a structured behavioral workflow with validated sleep diary inputs, guarded stage transitions, and a deterministic sleep-window calculator independent of the LLM. The proposal sets targets of at least 95% FSM transition accuracy and 100% prescription precision. Prescription precision refers to agreement with independently defined calculation outputs on the evaluated cases, rather than a claim of clinical accuracy across patients.

Objective 3 is to implement and evaluate a cognitive interrupt mechanism that detects relevant dysfunctional sleep beliefs, invokes the cognitive retrieval route, and restores the interrupted behavioral context. The proposal specifies interrupt precision of at least 80%, interrupt recall of at least 75%, and contextual recoverability of at least 90%. Positive, negative, and recovery scenarios will be used to assess these behaviors.

Objective 4 is to evaluate the integrated prototype through scripted dialogues and a controlled comparison with a linear LLM-RAG baseline, and to conduct a supplementary usability assessment of the interface. The architectural measures will be reported alongside observed failures and test conditions. The proposed usability assessment involves 5–10 peer volunteers completing scripted tasks and the System Usability Scale (SUS), with a target score above 68. These figures are planned evaluation targets, not achieved results.

## 1.4 Research Questions

The research questions translate the aim into observable architectural behaviors. They also define the evidence needed to determine whether the proposed integration addresses the problem statement.

RQ1: To what extent does hierarchical FSM orchestration preserve the specified behavioral progression under compliant inputs, conversational deviations, and contradictory diary inputs, compared with a linear LLM-RAG baseline?

RQ2: Can the integrated prototype reproduce independently specified sleep-window calculation outputs while keeping numerical decision-making outside the LLM and maintaining separation between behavioral and cognitive retrieval?

RQ3: How accurately does the cognitive interrupt mechanism distinguish relevant dysfunctional sleep beliefs from non-triggering messages, and how reliably does it restore the behavioral state and associated context after an interrupt?

RQ4: How do peer participants assess the usability of the prototype interface after completing scripted interactions, and what usability issues are identified for subsequent refinement?

RQ1 maps to FSM transition accuracy, RQ2 to calculation agreement and retrieval-isolation checks, and RQ3 to interrupt precision, recall, and contextual recoverability. RQ4 provides supplementary interface evidence through SUS responses and participant feedback. None of these questions measures the treatment effectiveness of the prototype.

## 1.5 Scope and Limitations

The project covers the design, implementation, and evaluation of a research prototype based on the architecture described in the FYP1 proposal. Its behavioral workflow contains four macro-states: baseline assessment, sleep restriction, stimulus control, and relapse prevention. These states represent the prototype's orchestration design. Their sequencing is not presented as a universal clinical schedule for all CBT-I programs. The implementation focuses on sleep diary management, rule-based sleep restriction guidance, and cognitive restructuring support within that workflow.

The technical scope includes a Python development environment, LangGraph orchestration, an open-source LLM, and a vector retrieval component such as FAISS. A behavioral protocol knowledge base and a cognitive intervention knowledge base provide separate content sources. The system also includes a deterministic calculator and checkpoint-based state persistence. Model selection, retrieval configuration, calculation rules, and transition conditions must be documented in the methodology so that the evaluation can be reproduced. No particular model or database configuration is claimed to have been implemented at this stage.

Evaluation follows the six scenario categories proposed in FYP1: workflow progression, workflow deviation, contradictory input, interrupt triggering, false-positive suppression, and post-interrupt recovery. Scripted dialogues exercise expected behaviors and selected failure cases. A baseline comparison retains the same model, knowledge content, and prompt assets while removing FSM routing. Additional checks examine retrieval boundaries and component integration. The supplementary peer study evaluates interaction with the interface; participants are not recruited to receive treatment or demonstrate symptom improvement.

The study does not include clinical diagnosis, medication management, emergency care, or deployment as a replacement for professional services. It does not measure remission, changes in insomnia severity, or other patient treatment outcomes. Any sleep-window output remains a research-prototype output and requires an appropriately reviewed protocol before clinical use. The local-model deployment proposed in FYP1 is a design intention; privacy depends on the actual inference, storage, logging, and networking configuration and cannot be inferred solely from the use of an open-source model.

The evaluation is limited by the number and diversity of scripted cases, the correctness of reference labels, and the coverage of the selected knowledge sources. Good performance on the test suite would not guarantee equivalent performance on unrestricted real-world conversations. Interrupt decisions can also depend on interpretation of a message, so ambiguous cases require documented labeling rules. The small convenience sample used for usability assessment limits the generalizability of interface findings. These limitations will be considered when interpreting results and recommending further development.

## 1.6 Significance and Expected Contributions

The expected software-engineering contribution is an inspectable separation of responsibilities within a conversational system. The FSM determines permitted workflow actions, the calculator supplies numerical outputs, retrieval provides task-relevant source material, and the LLM generates the conversational response. This separation is intended to make failures easier to locate and to reduce dependence on a single generated response for both dialogue and process control. Whether it delivers the intended benefit remains an empirical question for the evaluation.

A second expected contribution is a design for cognitive interruption without discarding behavioral progress. Checkpointed state and separate retrieval routes provide a concrete mechanism for suspending a task, addressing a relevant belief, and resuming the task with its data intact. Evaluating both triggering and recovery is important because a useful cognitive response alone would not demonstrate that the overall workflow remains consistent.

A third expected contribution is a reproducible evaluation package linking architectural components to explicit scenarios, reference outputs, and quantitative measures. The controlled baseline comparison is intended to clarify the effect of FSM routing within the selected configuration. Retrieval-isolation checks and failure analysis complement aggregate scores by identifying circumstances in which the architecture does not behave as intended. This evidence can inform later revisions of the prototype and further investigation of structured conversational applications.

The practical deliverables are the prototype, its behavioral and cognitive knowledge resources, documented calculation and transition rules, scenario scripts, and an evaluation report. Preliminary usability findings may guide improvements to the interface. Potential relevance to other structured support workflows is a direction for future investigation, rather than a demonstrated property of this study. The project makes no advance claim of improved clinical outcomes or reduced treatment dropout.

## 1.7 Overview of the Research Approach

The project adopts the Design Science Research approach described in FYP1, with iterative development organized through an Agile software development life cycle. The artifact is the hybrid RAG-FSM prototype. Requirements are derived from the selected CBT-I materials, the architectural concerns identified above, and the behaviors that the proposal intends to evaluate. Development proceeds through requirements analysis, architectural design, implementation, testing and refinement, and evaluation.

Before formal evaluation, expected transitions, calculation outputs, retrieval boundaries, and interrupt labels will be specified for the test cases. Integration testing will examine data movement between the diary, calculator, FSM, retrieval pipelines, and dialogue generator. Scenario-based evaluation will then compare the integrated architecture with the linear baseline under documented, comparable conditions. Quantitative results will be interpreted together with failed cases and the limitations of the test set. Any variability arising from model generation must be recorded when designing and reporting the comparison.

Usability assessment will supplement the architectural evaluation after the prototype can complete the scripted tasks. Participation should be voluntary and based on informed consent, with the research purpose and prototype limitations explained beforehand. Scripted or synthetic sleep information should be used wherever possible to avoid collecting unnecessary health information. The study should follow applicable institutional requirements before involving participants. Detailed procedures, instrumentation, and analysis methods will be specified in Chapter 3.
